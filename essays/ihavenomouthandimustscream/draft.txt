jouissance is Lacan's term — Žižek borrowed it and skewed it. It's a French word that literally means "enjoyment" but Lacan loaded it with a very specific meaning.
The basic idea: pleasure, as Lacan uses it, is regulated. Your body and mind seek a comfortable equilibrium — not too much stimulation, not too little. That's the pleasure principle. Jouissance is what happens when you push past that regulation into an intensity that is simultaneously pleasurable and painful. It's the point where enjoyment becomes so extreme it tips into suffering, but you keep going anyway.
The clinical examples are things like addiction — you know the substance is destroying you, but there's a compulsive pull toward it that isn't simply "I like how it feels." Or the person who keeps returning to a destructive relationship. Or self-sabotage. The question Lacan was trying to answer is: why do people repeatedly do things that clearly harm them, in ways that can't be explained by simple pleasure-seeking?
His answer was that there's this drive that operates beyond conscious pleasure, a kind of dark satisfaction that the person doesn't experience as enjoyment in any normal sense but that compels repetition anyway.
